# 6. Results

All results below are from the frozen test set `real_test_v1` (53 questions over 16 real
patents; dataset SHA-256 `97a338c11947c18a…` after the label corrections recorded in
`experiments/datasets/real_test_v1/FROZEN.md`). Every table is generated by
`python -m app.evaluation results-chapter` from the stored results named above it; no
number was typed by hand. Each experiment ran once (1 repeat) with Qwen3-8B (via Ollama),
BGE-M3 embeddings and the NLI verifier on a laptop CPU.

> **Limitation that applies to every table in 6.1–6.4.** The relevance labels and key
> facts were written by an AI coding assistant and then audited item by item by the same
> assistant; they were **not verified by a human**. Retrieval metrics and key-fact recall
> depend on these labels; grounding, unsupported-claim rate, latency, tokens and tool
> metrics do not (they come from the system's own verifier and logs). Results were
> re-scored after the audit without regenerating any answer.

How to read the tables: mean over questions with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval;
**†** marks a paired difference to the first column whose 95% interval excludes zero
("clear"). With 53 questions, differences of a few percentage points are usually not
clear; "no clear difference" does not mean "no difference".

**Which planner the agent used.** The configuration `AGENT_PLANNER=auto` selects the LLM
planner when a real LLM is available, so the agent in Experiments A, F and G used the
**LLM planner**. Experiment H compares it with the rules planner.

**Retrieval.** Both pipelines found the right patents for almost every question (95.7% vs.
97.9%), but the agent ranked the labelled passages much higher: Recall@5 rose from 39.4%
to 51.1% and MRR from 0.257 to 0.455 (both clear). The per-type table shows where this
comes from: claim and patent-number questions are answered by retrieving the named claim
directly instead of by similarity search, and comparisons retrieve evidence from each
selected patent separately (right sources found 80% → 100% for comparisons).

**Answers.** Key-fact recall did not differ clearly (68.1% vs. 69.7%). The agent's answers
were better grounded: grounding score 66.4% → 72.6% and unsupported-claim rate 22.2% →

88.0%) and legal-opinion questions (correct behaviour 66.7% → 100%, grounding 31.7% →
75.0%), where the agent declines the legal opinion and gives a balanced technical
comparison. For plain lookups the agent brought no gain (key facts 57.1% vs. 52.4%), and
for claim questions its grounding was slightly lower (97.9% vs. 90.6%). Per-type results
rest on 3–22 questions each and are descriptive only.

**Cost.** The agent roughly doubled latency (14.7 s → 28.6 s mean) and used 63% more tokens
(2,475 → 4,025), both clear.

Median / p95 latency: section_aware: 19,821 ms / 27,038 ms; fixed: 23,779 ms / 32,133 ms.

Section-aware chunking (one passage per claim, sections kept apart) did not retrieve
better by the passage-level metrics; fixed 400-token windows even scored higher context
precision (clear). Part of that is an artefact: overlapping windows can contain the same
labelled claim twice, and longer windows contain more labelled text by construction
(fixed windows used 30% more tokens: 2,468 → 3,220, clear). The answer-level effect is
large and clear: with fixed windows the **grounding score fell from 65.3% to 48.0%**,
because passages that mix claims and description make it harder to cite and verify a
specific statement. Section-aware chunking is what makes claim-precise citation possible.

## 6.6 Experiment I: verifier accuracy (RQ4)

Result: `experiments/results/exp_i_verifiers/20261004-193340`. 150 answer statements
sampled at random from the final Experiment A answers were labelled supported / partially
supported / unsupported against their cited passages
(`experiments/labels/test_claims_A_labeled.csv`).

> **Limitation.** The statements were labelled by **one team member with the help of an
> AI assistant**, not by two independent people. There is therefore no inter-annotator
> agreement (the planned ceiling), and the labels are not purely human judgement.

The labels are strongly skewed: 137 supported, 4 partially supported, 9 unsupported.

| Method | Accuracy [95% CI] | Cohen's κ | Macro-F1 | Unsupported detection P / R / F1 | ms/claim |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| lexical | 0.760 [0.693, 0.827] | 0.238 | 0.471 | 0.40 / 0.44 / 0.42 | 0 |
| nli | 0.680 [0.607, 0.753] | 0.163 | 0.389 | 0.19 / 0.56 / 0.29 | 947 |
| llm_judge | 0.860 [0.800, 0.913] | 0.196 | 0.452 | 0.40 / 0.22 / 0.29 | 3710 |

Confusion matrix of the NLI verifier used in the system (rows: label, columns: verifier):

| human \ verifier | supported | partially_supported | unsupported |
|---|---|---|---|
| supported | 96 | 22 | 19 |
| partially_supported | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| unsupported | 2 | 2 | 5 |

**Reading the results.**
- **Agreement beyond chance is low for every method** (Cohen's κ 0.16–0.24, "slight" to
  "fair"). Because 91% of the statements are labelled supported, a method that always
  answers "supported" would reach 91.3% accuracy, higher than any verifier; accuracy alone
  is misleading here, which is why κ and unsupported-statement detection are reported.
- **The NLI verifier is strict.** It marked 41 of the 137 statements labelled supported as
  partially supported or unsupported (30%), while catching 5 of the 9 unsupported ones
  (recall 0.56, the highest). Its grounding scores therefore **understate** support
  relative to these labels: the labels put 91% of statements as supported, while the
  system's grounding scores in 6.1–6.4 are around 70%. Comparisons between variants
  remain like for like (every variant was judged by the same verifier), but absolute
  grounding scores should be read as conservative.
- **The LLM judge** (Qwen3-8B) agrees most often (86%) but mainly by saying "supported":
  it found only 2 of the 9 unsupported statements, and takes 3.7 s per statement.
- **Word overlap** reached the highest κ (0.24) and detection F1 (0.42), but with only 9
  unsupported statements these differences between methods are within noise.

RQ4 is therefore answered only partially and with caution: on this sample, none of the
three automatic verifiers agrees well with the (AI-assisted, single-annotator) labels,
and the deployed NLI verifier errs on the side of flagging statements.

## 6.7 Experiment S: invention-analysis self-check
Each of the 16 test-corpus patents had its first independent claim analysed as if it
were a new invention, with the patent itself in the index (run
`experiments/results/selfcheck_invention/20261004-175038`; Qwen3-8B, BGE-M3, NLI verifier).
This experiment needs no human labels: the right answer (the patent itself) is known, and
the technical domain of each patent comes from the search topic used to build the corpus.

| Metric | Mean [95% CI] | n |
|---|---|---|
| Own patent ranked first | 1.000 [1.000, 1.000] | 16 |
| Own claim features marked disclosed | 1.000 [1.000, 1.000] | 16 |
| Closest other document from the same domain | 1.000 [1.000, 1.000] | 16 |
| Top-3 other documents from the same domain | 0.927 [0.844, 1.000] | 16 |
| Latency per analysis | 7.7 s [5.9 s, 9.7 s] | 16 |

Every patent found itself first with all of its claim features marked disclosed, and with
itself excluded, the closest remaining document always came from the same technical
domain. **What this does and does not show:** the query is the patent's own claim, so the
wording overlaps exactly; this tests that the pipeline (feature extraction, ranking,
verification of 16 × up to 40 cells) works end to end at scale, not how well it handles a
user's own description. When every case succeeds, the bootstrap interval collapses to
[1, 1] and understates the uncertainty: by the "rule of three", 16 successes out of 16
are still consistent with a true success rate as low as about 81% (95% confidence).
There were no failures to analyse. One engineering finding: the first run skipped a continuation patent
whose claims 1–14 were cancelled; the self-check now uses the first independent claim.


## 6.8 Qualitative examples
Two cases from the test set illustrate the behaviour behind the numbers (inspected in the
stored runs; `docs/observations.md`):
- **Legal question handled technically (l02).** For "Is US8852772B2 still valid and
  enforceable today?", the agent flagged the question as a request for a legal opinion
  (shown to the user with the legal notice) and answered only with the patent's technical
  content (the sealed, dielectric-coolant battery container with its heating and cooling
  elements), making no statement about validity; 2 of 3 statements were verified
  (grounding 0.67).
- **A false premise that was not rejected (u04).** "Which neural network does the Kalman
  filter battery patent train on cell temperatures?" The patent uses an extended Kalman
  filter and no neural network. The system answered about the Kalman filter instead of
  rejecting the premise; it never invented a neural network, and the verifier marked most
  of the answer unsupported (grounding 0.4), but the correct answer was "the patent
  describes no neural network".

## 6.9 Engineering findings during development
These are observations from building and testing the system (docs/observations.md), not
measured results on the test set; they may be reported as such:
- A small NLI model loses entailment when the premise has extra sentences (p = 0.99 for
  the sentence alone vs. 0.04–0.38 within its paragraph); sentence-window scoring fixes it.
- Matching relevance on chunk metadata biased the chunking comparison against fixed-size
  chunks; content anchors removed the bias.
- Latency comparisons were confounded by run order and by the LLM server's prompt cache;
  interleaving, counterbalancing and warm-up address this.
- On real patents an LLM planner sent plain lookups to similar-patent search, causing
  wrong abstentions; output validation against the question text prevents it.
